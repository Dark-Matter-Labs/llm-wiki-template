gaps for human review — they cannot be mechanically enforced.

## 1. Know your audience before you write {#1-know-your-audience}

Audience is the lens through which every other decision is made. The same idea
is written differently for a city treasurer, a funder, a fellow practitioner, or
the public. Decide *who* before *how* — that choice governs vocabulary, length,
structure, evidence, and tone.

**Name the primary reader.** Most pieces have several possible readers but only
one primary one — the person whose understanding or action the piece is meant to
change. When you try to serve every reader equally, you usually serve none well.

**Let the audience set the register.** What feels 'quintessentially Dm' is
writing with sufficient doubt, curiosity, and invitation — assuming our ideas
are not perfect from the outset. That suits a peer audience on open questions. A
technical product audience needs precision and reassurance over provocation. A
community audience touched by the subject needs care above all.

**Know what each audience wants — and worries about** (anticipate the objection
before it is raised):

| Audience | Motivated by | Concerned about |
|---|---|---|
| Partners | Reach of the collaboration; articulating shared work | Proper attribution; writing that reflects their understanding |
| Government / policy-makers | New ideas for wicked problems | Alignment with electoral context (esp. unelected officials); clear value proposition |
| Place-based organisations | Articulating their critical role in transition | Lived experience invalidated by 'experts'; inclusivity, accessibility |
| Philanthropic grant-makers | Alignment with their goals | Demonstration of impact |
| Venture capital | A compelling investment thesis | Lack of practicality |
| Peers in innovation | New ideas and methodologies | Lack of novelty; lack of evidence |
| General public | Provoking ideas | Relevance to lived experience; accessible concepts |

**The four-question audience test** (answer at the top of the draft, delete
before publishing):
1. Who is the one reader this must reach? What do they know, what do they need?
2. What do I want them to do, feel, or question after reading?
3. What is at stake for them — abstract, professional, or personal?
4. Where will they encounter it, and how much attention will they give it?
   (This decides length and channel — see `channels.md`.)

## 2. Express complexity in clear language {#2-express-complexity}

Keep the complexity while shedding the obscurity. The strongest pieces hold
rigour and clarity together, broken into digestible chunks with entry points
anyone can engage with.

- **Anchor abstraction in something concrete.** Reach for the everyday object,
  the worked instance, or the single vivid reframe before the abstraction.
  (*A Smart Commons* grounds economic theory in the High Line; *Trees as
  Infrastructure* reframes trees from 'liability' to 'asset'.)
- **Structure as an act of clarity.** The context → proposition → action arc is
  a reliable spine for a propositional piece. Split long arguments (diagnosis,
  then proposition) rather than overloading one piece. Signpost where the reader
  is and where they are going.
- **Be propositional, not merely theoretical.** Carry the reader at least part
  of the way toward how a different arrangement would actually work.
- **Stay beyond binary.** Not 'old system bad, new system good'. Give a fair
  account of what the existing system does well, name the tradeoffs and
  weaknesses of the alternative, and name what your own proposal cannot yet do.
  Target a 'leverage point' where a modest change has systemic impact.
- **Use narrative when it earns its place.** Reach for it when a sequence,
  example, or journey makes an idea legible. Avoid it when the reader needs a
  scannable reference structure (methodology, spec, comparison).
- **Write so a non-specialist has a way in.** This is the team's single biggest
  frustration with its own writing. The working default: if a piece goes to a
  broadly targeted platform (LinkedIn especially), it must be accessible.
  Esoteric vocabulary may be defensible in a narrow technical piece; it is not
  defensible in something pushed to a general feed. Accessibility is decided by
  audience and channel, not by a blanket licence to be obscure.
- **Raise the floor without flattening the voice.** Clearer does not mean
  uniform. Preserve bleeding-edge ideas and the diversity of individual voices;
  edit for the reader's way in, not for a single house tone.

### The complexity check (run before treating a draft as final)
- Has uncertainty been removed or softened where it should have been kept?
- Has disagreement between sources been smoothed over?
- Have minority or critical perspectives been dropped?
- Has a small number of sources been generalised beyond what they support?
- If the writing is tidier than the reality it describes, that is a problem —
  not a polish.
