## 3. Coined terms and situating our work {#3-coined-terms}

How we name things signals where we stand in the wider conversation. **Default
to established terminology** — it connects the work to prior research and
practice and signals that proposals are credible rather than freshly invented in
isolation. (Consistent with the Attribution Guide: credit external frameworks,
do not present them as Dm's own.)

**Plug into existing discourse — propositionally.** Engage serious academic
discourse directly, then put it to work — neither ignoring scholarship nor
disappearing into specialist exegesis. (*Radicle Civics* names its lineage —
Latour, Easterling — and uses each to motivate a concrete claim. The *Living
Stewardship Agreement* sets Ostrom, Wenar, Arendt, Locke to work and is precise
about where it departs from each, then carries the argument into a working
prototype and real places.)

Two habits make this work:
1. Cite the discourse you are entering — and mark where you part from it — so
   peers can see the work is grounded. (Peers and policy-makers care most about
   novelty and evidence; visible, accurate engagement answers both.)
2. Do not let the citation become the point. Deploy the idea toward something
   practical and keep it readable to a non-specialist. This is how Dm writing is
   distinct from academic writing.

**Situate in our own work too.** Cross-reference other Dm work — it builds
coherence without centralisation. When you use a Dm concept, link to where it
was developed, as you would an external source. The Dm4 Glossary is the shared
reference and is *evolving* — it expects to change, invites disagreement, and is
not a fixed authority. Check it first; contribute back when your work sharpens a
definition.

The glossary marks how settled each term is, and this maturity matters for style:
- **Anchored** (defined, core to how we work): use directly and consistently,
  with a link for the fuller definition.
- **Evolving** (in active use, still being aligned): still needs situating in
  the piece itself.
- **Emerging** (early-stage or borrowed, not yet settled): situate explicitly;
  the reader cannot assume shared meaning yet.

Once a Dm term is coined and settled, **use it consistently** rather than
inventing a fresh near-synonym each time.

### When to coin a new term
The rule is not 'avoid new terms' — some of Dm's most recognised work is built
on coinages (Smart Commons, Trees as Infrastructure, Radicle Civics,
Many-to-Many, Life-Ennobling Economics). The rule is that a new term must be
**earned**. Coining a term for something that already has an established name
increases scepticism among the very experts whose credibility we want — it reads
as either not knowing the field or asserting a claim on it through rebranding.

Coin only when an existing term would actively distort the point — when the
available vocabulary carries assumptions you are arguing against, or has no word
for the thing you are describing. Before coining, check the concept does not
already have a name in the literature.

**Say why you are coining it.** The text itself must explain what existing
vocabulary fails to capture and what your term does instead. Then anchor the new
term immediately in a concrete referent or established concept.

### Three tests before you coin
- **Distortion test**: would an existing, established term mislead the reader or
  smuggle in an assumption I am arguing against? If no established term misleads,
  use the established term.
- **Justification test**: does my text say, in plain words, what existing
  vocabulary fails to capture and why this new term is needed? If not, add the
  justification or drop the coinage.
- **Anchor test**: have I tied the new term to a concrete example or recognised
  concept so a non-specialist can grasp it? If it floats free, it will not
  travel.

**Build the ecosystem, not just the brand.** Cite generously; link to originals;
name the source of any framework that is not ours. New terminology should extend
a conversation others can join, not enclose it.

## 4. Worldview, positionality, and care {#4-positionality}

Dm writes largely from UK, EU, North American, and occasionally South Korean
contexts. The honest response is not to claim a neutral or universal vantage
point we do not occupy, but to be explicit about positionality — say where a
piece is written from, and avoid presenting a situated argument as though it held
everywhere. The failure to guard against is the quiet universalising of a
particular, largely Anglo-European worldview.

**Declaring positionality is the working default for public-facing work — but it
is a judgment, not an automatic rule.** A degree of ambiguity can be a legitimate
strategic choice (letting an argument be weighed on its own terms before a reader
files the author into a camp). Weigh that against the cost: not declaring can
read as hiding a bias. The balance shifts with audience — strongest case to
declare for a broad public audience (least context to place the work); more can
be left implicit for a specialist or internal audience. Make the choice
deliberately, not by accident.

### Positionality can carry personal risk — weigh it, do not carry it alone
On contested geopolitical or identity questions, declaring who you are can expose
the author to targeted harassment. (One Dm piece on an India–Pakistan water
dispute declared the authors' nationalities as a deliberate move and drew
sustained, personally distressing trolling.) The lesson is not 'never declare' —
it is that the writer disclosing something about themselves bears a real and
uneven cost, which should be named before publication, not discovered after. The
same applies to crediting a contributor — **always seek consent before
crediting**.

Two things follow:
1. Treat a personal disclosure on a sensitive topic as raising the stakes (in
   the Publishing Guide's sense) — flag it for review and decide, with others,
   whether and how to declare.
2. This is a shared responsibility, not a burden for the individual author. How
   much risk a person takes on, and how much Dm holds with them, is decided
   together. If declaring would expose someone to harm they have not chosen,
   that is a reason to reconsider the framing, channel, or timing.

### Working with Indigenous partners
This guide is not a manual for Indigenous contexts and should not be read as one.
Three things hold:
1. **Be careful what can and cannot be shared.** Sensitive traditional knowledge
   and cultural IP are not ours to publish; the default when unsure is to
   withhold and ask.
2. **Defer to established frameworks** rather than improvising — OCAP®
   (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession; First Nations Information Governance
   Centre) and the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective
   Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics; Global Indigenous Data
   Alliance) for data and knowledge; Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) for
   participation and publication.
3. **Let Indigenous partners set the terms** of how their knowledge is
   represented, attributed, and shared.

### Language, translation, and local reach
Almost all output is in English, which excludes some audiences. Translation is
not always feasible, but ask whether a key piece should be translated (*Trees as
Infrastructure* was published in Japanese and Mandarin). Platform popularity
varies by country (Medium is barely used in South Korea and Japan), so a local
actor sharing work on familiar platforms brings benefits beyond language alone.
